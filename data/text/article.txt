# Europe’s Shared Freight Experiment Delivers Uneven Results as Partners Weigh Expansion

ROTTERDAM, Netherlands — A nine-month experiment intended to make European retail deliveries cheaper and more predictable has produced sharply different results for its corporate participants, complicating negotiations over a second round of funding.

The North Sea Distribution Compact, an €84 million initiative involving public authorities and private businesses, connected purchasing forecasts, warehouse schedules and freight bookings across six countries. An independent review released Thursday examined operations serving 240 stores and 18 distribution depots. Its central finding was that savings depended heavily on how companies adapted their own operations.

Carrefour emerged with lower waste and stronger store margins. DHL Group accumulated delivery penalties after struggling to meet revised schedules. Microsoft secured additional business, although customers questioned whether some of its software savings justified unexpectedly expensive integration work. Meanwhile, a contract change by Amazon left other participants debating what the online retailer intended to do next.

The results matter beyond the participating depots. Regional authorities must decide within six weeks whether to release another €46 million for expansion, while corporate partners are negotiating who should bear the costs when a shared forecast produces an unsuccessful delivery.

For Carrefour, the review supplied a straightforward argument for continuing. Across 62 participating stores, discarded fresh food fell 18 percent against a comparable group of outlets, while the share of advertised products available on shelves increased. Operating margins at those locations rose by 0.6 percentage points after programme fees.

The French retailer attributed much of the improvement to smaller, more frequent deliveries arranged using pooled demand forecasts. Store managers could reduce orders before excess stock arrived rather than mark down products already approaching their expiry dates.

“We used to discover the mistake when we opened the back-room door,” said Marion Leclerc, Carrefour’s fictional regional operations director. “Now we can correct an order while it is still a number on a screen.” The company has approved funding to extend the system to another 90 stores.

DHL’s experience was more difficult. The review found that 13.4 percent of its deliveries in the pilot missed their agreed windows, compared with 6.1 percent on comparable routes outside the programme. Several depots received revised manifests too late to reorganise driver shifts, producing queues in the morning and partially filled vehicles later in the day.

The logistics group incurred €4.8 million in penalties and emergency staffing expenses. Two participating retail contracts will be reassigned when their current terms expire. Although other operators encountered the same forecasting changes, reviewers said DHL had underestimated the staffing required to handle repeated adjustments.

In a written response, DHL acknowledged that its implementation had fallen short of contractual requirements. It has withdrawn its original timetable for adding three depots. For the existing sites, the company must now submit weekly recovery reports before programme administrators will approve additional routes.

Microsoft Corporation supplied much of the cloud infrastructure linking orders, warehouse records and delivery appointments. According to the review, automated processing reduced the time required to reconcile a shipment across different systems from several hours to about 20 minutes. The company also signed three additional service agreements arising from the pilot.

Those gains came with a substantial qualification. Customers collectively spent €11 million more than initially budgeted on connecting older systems to Microsoft’s platform. At four smaller operators, those costs absorbed the entire first-year saving from reduced administrative work. Their finance teams have asked for revised pricing before agreeing to extend their contracts.

Microsoft said the overruns reflected inconsistent historical data and requirements added after installation began. It offered service credits and a fixed-price migration package for the next phase. Programme officials welcomed the faster processing but said expansion contracts would need clearer limits on implementation charges.

Some investments in physical equipment produced less contested returns. Schneider Electric installed energy-management systems at seven depots, where electricity consumption per handled pallet fell 21 percent after adjustment for seasonal demand. All seven operators renewed their service agreements, and the company received €6.2 million in follow-on orders. Siemens also secured additional orders after its loading-bay controls reduced equipment stoppages during the trial.

The picture was considerably weaker for Maersk. Its proposed combination of sea freight and scheduled inland transfers repeatedly broke down when vessels arrived outside the narrow windows reserved at participating terminals. Containers then waited for replacement onward services, missing the store replenishment cycle the compact was designed to support.

Maersk absorbed storage charges and issued customer rebates worth €3.1 million. The review said its dedicated service had cost more per completed shipment than the conventional arrangements it replaced. The shipping company has suspended that service while renegotiating terminal access, leaving its planned expansion without an agreed starting date.

For SAP, commercial progress was accompanied by operational trouble. Its order-management tools shortened purchasing approval times by almost a third, and two participants signed multiyear extensions. But an error in the treatment of amended orders generated duplicate entries, forcing finance teams to investigate thousands of records before closing their quarterly accounts.

SAP provided a correction and assigned additional support staff, but affected customers incurred overtime and outside accounting costs. One customer has made its renewal conditional on reimbursement. The programme’s review credited the faster purchasing process while identifying the duplicate records as a significant control failure.

Unilever likewise recorded benefits that came with a measurable cost. Consolidated deliveries reduced its transport spending on participating routes by 8 percent, but catalogue-matching errors temporarily removed several products from store ordering systems. The company estimated that the resulting lost sales exceeded its freight savings during the affected quarter. It is retaining the delivery arrangement while rebuilding the catalogue interface.

Tesco ended its trial of a separate stock-reallocation module after inaccurate recommendations sent slow-selling goods to stores already carrying excess inventory. The retailer wrote down £2.6 million of stock and requested repayment of implementation fees. Its operations team will use its previous allocation process for the coming trading period.

Other participants had narrower, descriptive roles in the review. Procter & Gamble supplied product dimensions and packaging codes for the common shipment records. Nestlé submitted temperature-handling specifications for designated product categories. IBM documented the format used to exchange order messages between the programme’s central registry and participating warehouse systems.

The unresolved question drawing the most attention, however, concerns Amazon. Halfway through the pilot, it changed 40 percent of its reserved warehouse capacity from guaranteed availability to an “on-request” arrangement. The published contract amendment contains no explanation of the commercial reasoning, and the review does not disclose utilisation or profitability figures for that capacity.

Some programme officials interpreted the change as preparation for a more flexible expansion, allowing Amazon to move space between participating merchants as demand shifted. Others thought it might indicate reluctance to commit resources to a business whose returns remained unclear. Neither interpretation could be tested against the released figures.

Amazon said the amendment aligned contractual terms with its operating model but declined to provide volume forecasts. Its payments to the programme continued on the existing schedule. Negotiators said they could not yet determine whether its next commitment would cover substantially more warehouse space or considerably less.

Walmart introduced a similar uncertainty through a different contractual change. The retailer replaced an option to book weekly delivery slots with a right to negotiate larger quarterly blocks. The document specifies the booking process but withholds minimum volumes, cancellation provisions and prices.

A quarterly arrangement could support a broader commitment if Walmart expects sufficient demand. It could also allow the company to postpone decisions that previously required weekly reservations. Asked which interpretation reflected its intentions, Walmart said its assessment was continuing. Programme administrators have excluded the proposed volumes from their expansion forecasts.

Alibaba’s proposed merchant-consolidation service remains difficult to assess for another reason. Its fee depends on warehouse utilisation, but the published proposal redacts the threshold governing standby charges. Without that figure, reviewers could not establish whether the proposed terms offered an attractive return or would leave the company struggling to cover reserved capacity.

These gaps have become central to the funding discussions. Public authorities want binding delivery commitments before financing additional depots. Several corporate participants want those depots confirmed before agreeing to the volumes that would make them economical. The review recommends phased contracts, with spending released only after specified capacity has been booked.

At Thursday’s meeting, Carrefour presented its expansion schedule while DHL submitted a revised recovery plan. Microsoft circulated new pricing terms. Amazon’s capacity amendment remained on the agenda for further discussion. The compact’s next phase will depend on whether those separate negotiations can produce a workable network—and whether enough participants can demonstrate that joining it improves their own business.
